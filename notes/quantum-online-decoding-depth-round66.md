# AlphaQubit 2: a real throughput advance and the remaining online-control boundary

AlphaQubit 2 changes the evidence: learned decoding is no longer fairly described here as only accurate but too slow. The selected paper reports real accelerator throughput and pacing-based streaming tests. It also explicitly excludes live measurement/communication hardware from its latency evaluation. Those facts support a narrower and more useful systems conclusion than either complete fault-tolerant operation or dismissal as an offline-accuracy demonstration.

The initial census found the generic Willow distinction already closed: MHW 07/quantum 3 separate neural accuracy, classical online decoding and latency versus backlog. I did not reread that as new work. The first targeted discovery exposed a newer same-platform component. Before opening a paper, I recorded the selection change to [AlphaQubit 2,arXiv2512.07737v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.07737v2),revised 11 March 2026. One query and two official surfaces—metadata and exact methods—were used;the unused third slot was not filled with another paper. No assets, SDK, model, data, code, quantum service or native experiment was acquired or run.

The important capability points must stay separate. Full AQ2's lowest simulated error rates at large distances are not the same operating point as AQ2-RT's faster intermediate-distance result. The paper uses physical Willow data to validate adapted decoding accuracy, and commercial Trillium hardware to measure compute speed. These are substantial positives. They are not an integrated live-QPU logical-computation demonstration. At larger distances, the faster variant is explicitly insufficiently accurate, and further co-design or dedicated hardware remains future work. [Main §§3–6;Methods A5–A7](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.07737v2).

The timing protocol is stronger than a batch-only speed number. It buffers cycles while processing blocks, introduces simulated periodic arrivals, and measures the delay from the last cycle to the final prediction. Its reported decoder latency does not grow with experiment duration at the selected points. That is evidence against an unstable decoder queue under those tested conditions. It is not a direct test of qubit readout, serialization, transport, jitter, dropped messages or the deadline of a particular logical algorithm. The authors retain scheduling-related tail outliers and describe a dedicated real-time OS as a future mitigation, not a demonstrated fix.

Throughput, final-answer latency and a feedback deadline therefore remain different requirements. A streaming implementation can keep up on average while its last-answer delay still matters to an algorithm. Conversely, latency longer than one correction cycle does not automatically invalidate the pipeline. It is also wrong to require a physical correction command after every cycle by assumption:the decoder's observable/Pauli-frame contract and the logical operation determine when its answer is needed.

Data and outcome units remain equally important. The paper separates simulated scaling data, model-selection/development sets, held-out tests and adapted physical-device data. It includes a genuine held-out evaluation safeguard rather than simply selecting on the final test. It derives per-cycle logical error from shot outcomes or a duration fit;that is a modelled statistic distinct from a raw per-experiment failure fraction. The underlying raw-device acceptance/discard and preprocessing ledger was not acquired here. A no-match postselection search cannot certify that every original hardware attempt was retained.

Different code cycles also need matching meaning. The Bell-flagged colour representation combines sequential basis subcycles;it cannot be compared to surface/superdense timing as though every cycle had identical physical duration. The reported very-low-error scaling points are simulated under a specified anticipated noise regime, not observed large-distance hardware performance or a complete application result.

The classical alternatives are serious. The paper compares multiple matching and search decoders, including maintained PyMatching, and identifies useful speed/accuracy trade-offs. Hardware differs across those system points:TPU, local CPU and cloud CPU timing are not a matched energy, cost or intrinsic algorithm-speed experiment. Large-distance baseline extrapolation and a described implementation bottleneck also limit a claim of permanent neural dominance. The new result is nevertheless counterevidence to a permanent classical/manual-software moat.
