# UK AI Labour Market Survey 2025: methods, denominators and career interpretation

Checked 20 October 2026 against material published by 19 October. Existing labor, UK-access and Skills England references were read first. This pass audits the complete public methods and relevant findings in [DSIT 2026/002, *AI Labour Market Survey 2025*](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6960ef384343a0da370869b7/AI_Labour_Market_Survey_2025_report.pdf), produced by Gardiner & Theobald (G&T), [published 28 January 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-labour-market-survey-2025-report). The retained PDF has 50 pages; six relevant pages were rendered and visually checked. This is a primary-methods audit, not respondent-data replication or a new vacancy sample.

**The report establishes what a small, selected group of AI-active organisations said about recruiting, skills and training. It does not estimate a representative UK vacancy shortage, a wage premium or an attainable opening for an India-based developer.** Its detailed findings are useful for forming specific hypotheses about practical experience, internal transition and training. The national-sounding summary needs narrower denominators and several explicit corrections.

## Population, recruitment and nonresponse

Methods pp. 5–6 describe a **self-selective online survey launched in January 2025**, commissioned in December 2024. Invitations went to **3,940 organisations**, selected from commercial channels such as Crown Commercial Services, self-identification as an AI provider and DSIT identification of relevant organisations. **119 responded**, approximately **3%**; **20 interviews** supplement the survey. The 2020 comparison had 118 survey responses and 50 interviews.

This is not a probability sample drawn from a documented complete register of all employers. Frame completeness, duplicate/eligibility screening, respondents' job functions, exact closing date, recruitment reminders, nonrespondent characteristics and contact/disposition counts are not supplied in the inspected report. No poststratification weights, nonresponse model, design effects, confidence intervals or formal significance-testing procedure are documented. Absence of a documented procedure is not proof of every implementation choice, but it prevents claiming population-representative precision. Similar response counts and response rates in 2020 do not establish comparable selection or a longitudinal panel.

The composition is consequential: **78 of 119** are commercial AI developers in a later group table; approximately **66%** overall are Group 1, with internally developing and third-party-using organisations forming Groups 2 and 3. **111 of 119 are SMEs**: 54 micro, 43 small, 14 medium; only **8 are large**. Greater London contributes **59**, South East **22**, South West **10**. Thus small and regional cross-tabs can depend on few firms, while role totals may depend heavily on the eight large respondents. Employer-weighted and employee-weighted quantities should not be interchanged.

Questions were optional and figure-specific response counts vary. G&T says it did not retain the collected data. That does not establish that no other authorized custodian retains it. No public questionnaire, respondent-level file, analysis code, weighting specification or full cross-tabulation accompanies the inspected publication. Its bibliography and glossary are not a technical replication appendix.

## What counts as AI work or a shortage

Group 1 develops proprietary AI solutions for market; Group 2 develops internal tools, possibly using third-party models; Group 3 uses/procures others' tools. The population therefore spans AI producers and users. It is not exclusively frontier-model research or generative-agent employers.

The glossary defines an **AI role** by models, tools or technologies being a core part of the job. Its Turing-framework categories distinguish professionals whose core responsibilities concern data/AI, leaders responsible for procurement/governance, and broader workers whose jobs may be affected. Those are not standardized vacancy titles or guaranteed seniority/degree equivalents. Indeed, the report acknowledges that “senior” can mean different things in a small firm and an executive-led large company.

The **97% skills-gap** headline, based on 119 respondents, asks about at least one gap **in the AI labour market**. It is not 97% of all UK employers, 97% of workers failing a test or 97% with a funded unfilled role. Multiple-response items include understanding AI concepts/algorithms (**60%**), data management (**38%**) and programming/statistics (**36% each**). A separate **28%** say existing employees' technical gaps prevented meeting business goals. These are perceptions and self-attributions; there is no observed productivity counterfactual or tested competence distribution.

“Hard to fill” is employer-identified. The inspected methods do not specify a common vacancy-duration, attempted-search, wage-adjustment or demonstrated-candidate threshold. A perceived shortage may combine specialized skill needs, experience, offered pay, working conditions, location and screening practices. The survey cannot identify their causal contributions or prove a market-wide excess demand at prevailing wages.

## Vacancies, recruitment and intentions: keep the bases visible

| Published measure | Actual base/object | Responsible reading |
|---|---|---|
| 58% had an AI vacancy since 2023 | Figure 6, **n=119**, retrospective two-year history | Not the current vacancy rate, number of vacancies, accepted starts or unfilled stock |
| 35% found at least one vacancy hard to fill | Figure 7, **n=66**, despite nearby prose saying “all organisations” | Conditional/base ambiguity remains; do not relabel as 35% of all UK organisations |
| 31% experience / 30% technical skills among reasons | Figures 9–10, **n=64**, multi-reason responses | Not candidate failure rates or exclusive causal shares; pay competition also appears |
| 44% professional / 42% leader / 14% worker | Figure 8, **n=70** caption | Seniority composition of reported hard-to-fill responses, not vacancy probability for each worker tier |
| 57% plan agentic AI within three years | Figure 5, **n=119** | Employer intention, not approved headcount, purchasing budget, realized deployment or 2031 forecast |

The Figure 7/8/9 bases—66, 70 and 64—cannot be reconciled to one conditional recruitment funnel from the published questionnaire information. This is an explicit documentation gap, not a reason to invent missing records or declare every percentage false. A numerator/count definition and questionnaire routing would resolve it.

The report compares 2020 and 2025 percentages and frequently calls changes “significant.” It provides no inferential tests for that wording in the inspected methods. In several places it says hiring difficulty **fell** from 69% to 35%, and technical-skill reasons fell from 65% to 30%, even while the executive narrative emphasizes increasing recruitment pressure. These are different measures and selected cross-sections, not an identified national trend. A new broader AI definition and changing producer/user mix can also alter the comparison. Claims that improved education caused a decline are interpretations rather than tested effects.

No quantitative salary distribution, accepted compensation, vacancy-duration distribution or longitudinal employee earnings is supplied. Sixteen of 64 reason respondents cite low pay/benefits or excessive salary demands. Interview accounts of retention competition are useful hypotheses, not a measured salary premium or proof that every shortage converts to a high-paying job.

## Entry pathways and qualifications: organisations versus people

Figure 13 (**n=114**) reports organisations using each pathway: prior AI roles **77%**, prior non-AI roles **74%**, career starters **60%**, apprentices **19%**, internships **18%**. These options are not mutually exclusive. An employer using graduates does not imply that 60% of its vacancies or hires are graduate opportunities. Prior non-AI work also does not reveal the entrants' earlier technical depth, conversion success or required experience.

Figure 15 separately describes the sampled AI workforce: graduates **36%**, professionals new to AI **35%**, apprentices **3%**, interns **1%**, other **25%**. The aggregation and treatment of employees across firms need raw implementation to reproduce. These are workforce pathway stocks, not necessarily current hiring flows. The size and regional tables also lack enough cell counts/weighting detail to generalize a national entry route from them; only eight responding employers are large.

Two summary errors should remain visible. The [official HTML executive summary](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-labour-market-survey-2025-report/ai-labour-market-survey-2025-report-executive-summary) calls apprenticeships **19% of AI hires**. The main report explicitly distinguishes **19% of organisations using apprenticeships** from **3% of their AI workforce**. Similarly, the summary says **13% of graduate schemes include AI training**, whereas Figure 28's base is **119 organisations**, and its category is training undertaken as part of a graduate programme. Neither number supplies the summary's graduate-scheme denominator.

Education measures likewise concern respondent organisations/occupants, rather than audited vacancy requirements. Figure 23 (**n=119**) displays PhD **53%** and master's **84%** under a “demand” title, while the section introduces qualifications of people occupying AI roles. The original questionnaire is needed to settle the exact requirement/presence wording. Figure 24/25 (**n=114**) clearly describe organisations with relevant qualifications: computer science **76%**, data science **66%**, engineering **64%**, social sciences **37%**. These multi-response proportions do not imply that 84% of all AI jobs require a master's, that a particular degree causes hiring, or that social-science qualifications replace technical prerequisites in every role.

This preserves both observations: advanced qualifications are common in these organisations, and several routes/backgrounds coexist. It supports inspecting the actual requirement of a reachable role. It does not justify buying a degree or certificate without a specific access or competence gap.

## Training: common activity, unmeasured effectiveness

Training includes structured courses/workshops and informal reading, mentoring or workplace learning. In 2025 all 119 respondents report some training, **88% informal**, **52% formal**, **40% both**. These are compatible overlapping categories. The HTML summary's “informal instead of structured” wording should not become an exclusive choice. Neither training completion nor prevalence establishes independent retained skill, productivity or wage returns.

Interviews favor combining foundations with practical commercial work, mentorship and exposure to customer environments. These mechanisms are plausible and actionable to test, but no assignment or outcome evaluation establishes which training format works best. A further source inconsistency is visible in Figure 27: its legend/data show MOOC participation **22% in 2020 and 17% in 2025**, opposite the adjacent prose's increase from 17% to 22%. No trend inference should select one version without reconciliation.

The useful preparation implication is to demonstrate the specific capability behind a stated gap: reliable data handling, an explainable model/evaluation choice, deployment under constraints or an independently reviewed commercial result. The existing transfer/selection gates supply the test. The report does not establish an optimal number of study hours, a causal certificate return or guaranteed leadership progression.

## UK-facing work from India: an access question remains

Figure 16's **38%** concerns **69 responding organisations** that say they have hired someone from outside the UK. It is not 38% of all UK firms, sponsors, vacancies or currently overseas-accessible positions. The question is past-tense, while accompanying prose shifts to current recruiting. Employment abroad, relocation to the UK, nationality and visa sponsorship are different states; the available data do not establish their split.

Reasons for international recruitment have **n=27**; six cite skill availability, five best candidate and five cost. Obstacles have **n=13**: six mention visas, three cost and two security clearance. These small, selected responses show obstacles worth checking; they cannot estimate the probability of sponsorship or remote eligibility. Published interview opinions—including claims about visa limits—are not immigration rules and are not used as legal facts here.

For an India-based developer serving UK clients, the valid sequence is still: identify the actual employing/contracting entity and permitted country, inspect the work/clearance requirements, demonstrate the demanded responsibility and then evaluate feasible access. A UK AI skills gap does not remove those gates. No new personal nationality, qualifications, tenure, immigration eligibility or hiring odds are inferred.

## Update search and remaining evidence gaps

A bounded official-title search and the publication page did not locate a superseding **AI Labour Market Survey 2026** report. That is a search result, not proof of absence. A [different UK AI sectoral-analysis survey](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/uk-artificial-intelligence-sectoral-analysis-survey) has a May 2026 fieldwork notice, with Ipsos/Perspective Economics and a business-sector frame. It is not a revised G&T labor report or a new validated shortage estimate. No fieldwork notice was counted as findings.

The main missing artifacts are the questionnaire/routing, eligible recruitment frame and response dispositions, weights/nonresponse analysis, organization/worker aggregation rules, cross-year comparability, item counts/cross-tabs, vacancy/search/pay outcomes and retained respondent data/code. A stronger shortage conclusion would need stable definitions, representative or transparently corrected samples and actual vacancies/hiring/compensation. It would weaken if representative replication or clearer denominators remove the pattern. A stronger personal route still requires actual transferable work and reachable entry conditions.

The source/claim matrix preserves these distinctions and the source hash. Government publication does not mean random sampling, and the HTML explicitly attributes findings/recommendations to G&T rather than government policy. No private records, applications, outreach, browser downloads, installations or shared-file changes occurred.
