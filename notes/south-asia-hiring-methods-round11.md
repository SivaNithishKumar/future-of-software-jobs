# India and South Asia: observed hiring signals and their limits

Checked 20 October 2026 against public material published by the research cutoff of 19 October. This pass reads the existing India-services, IMF-methods and labor-baseline reports, then audits three primary research families: the World Bank's October 2025 South Asia report, its October 2026 regional chapter with India survey documentation, and the June 2026 author version of *AI and Services-Led Growth*. No new employer sample or issuer-headcount survey was collected.

**The geography bridge is real but narrower than “AI creates or destroys Indian developer jobs.”** Indian online advertisements directly show changing demand for AI skills. A 2026 establishment survey directly measures reported AI use. These improve on projecting US patterns alone. However, the job-ad estimates still use US-derived task/exposure measures, selected employers and advertised salaries. They do not observe successful hires, realized pay or individual returns to training. The newer regional work also supplies a counterchannel: more AI-exposed firms can add international supplier relationships while changing their mix of vacancies.

The 2026 regional chapter's firm-level job-ad panel is not a refresh of the 2025 occupational displacement estimate. Its approximately 25m ads and different starting date, firm linkage and sample filters should not be spliced to the earlier approximately 28m series as a trend. Likewise, an author page updated in September does not date its June PDF's data to September.

## 1. The 2025 report: Indian demand observations, imported exposure structure

Annex 2.2, printed pp. 43–45, distinguishes worker surveys from advertisements. The labor-force exercise covers five regional countries and 25 other emerging economies. Its India row is **311,661 working-age observations from 2023**; Nepal's is 2017 and Bhutan's 2024. These are different survey vintages, not a synchronized regional employment panel. Four-digit US SOC exposure scores are averaged through an ISCO crosswalk. India's survey occupations are only three-digit, leaving another aggregation step to reproduce.

“Exposure” measures overlap between occupational abilities and AI capabilities. “Complementarity” is constructed from O*NET work contexts such as responsibility, communication, criticality and physical conditions. Multiplying exposure by one minus that complementarity score produces a substitution-oriented index. These are classifications, not observed replacement decisions or probabilities that a particular Indian worker will lose a job. The reported 7% of regional employment classified as highly exposed with low complementarity is neither an observed layoff rate nor a forecast of 7% unemployment. The report itself calls exposure assessments speculative even after complementarity adjustment.

**The ad sample is unusually relevant to a formal software worker, and unusually unsuitable for generalizing to all workers.** Annex Table A2.3 puts professionals at 51.47% of Lightcast observations versus 5.67% of the labor-force distribution, and managers at 22.02% versus 2.64%. Almost all advertisements are urban; the vast majority are Indian. Exact country shares, geographic reweighting and an India-only regression are not supplied in the inspected methods. Thus “South Asian” pooled results may largely reflect Indian formal recruiting, while remaining poor estimates for smaller regional markets, informal work or the entire Indian labor force.

The public [2025 chart workbook](https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/029dbb0faf2410c6530b32d58325ecc5-0310012025/related/SADU-October-2025-Charts-Chapter-2.xlsx) makes one date issue clearer. Sheet `2.8A` reports AI-related ad shares of **2.89% in January 2023 and 6.51% in March 2025**. Therefore the main text's March endpoint has direct aggregate support; the figure caption and Annex's February endpoint are inconsistent with that series. This does not resolve the exact estimation sample or vintage. Shares can rise through more AI ads, fewer other ads or both; they are not successful AI hires or a stock of AI-employed people.

### Offered pay is a selected association

Only **16% of ads disclose salary**. Annex Table A2.6's most controlled column uses **3,180,122** observations, with country-month, city and occupation fixed effects; errors cluster by occupation. Its AI coefficient is **0.276 (SE 0.055)** in log advertised salary. The workbook displays 27.6 and the text rounds to a 28% premium. That is a log-point approximation, not an exact percentage transformation or a measured personal raise.

These are additive fixed effects, not every employer, occupation, city and time interaction held constant. Seniority, experience, firm, salary-disclosure behavior and job quality may still differ within those comparisons. Missing offered pay may be selective and change over time. No worker is randomly assigned a skill or credential. Neither the approximate 28% figure nor the older platform's 13–17% conditional premium estimates the return to purchasing a course. Actual earnings in the labor-force exercise and salaries advertised online are separate data objects.

### The ChatGPT design requires a counterfactual, not just a launch date

The baseline outcome is log occupation-month listing counts, with occupation and month effects and a post-November-2022 indicator interacted with occupational exposure. Complementarity quartiles add interactions. Table A2.7 contains **17,795 occupation-month observations**, not 28m independent treatment units. Errors cluster by occupation. Entry-level estimates group ads requiring **0–5 years**, which includes experienced workers; these are not cohorts of actual graduates or first jobs. Business services is a list of occupations assisted by ChatGPT-4o and manually revised, not solely an employer-industry category. Software developers are one listed occupation, not the entire estimated treatment group.

An unexpected global launch does not randomize occupations. Differential post-pandemic hiring normalization, the global technology investment cycle, financing conditions, client demand, offshoring and changing platform coverage can correlate with exposure. Occupation effects remove stable differences and month effects remove common shocks; they do not remove every exposure-correlated trend. Low-exposure controls can also be affected through demand, wages, outsourcing and mobility. Ads can respond before measured deployment, while firms can use purchased AI without hiring an AI specialist.

The chart data enable a more concrete pretrend audit. In sheet `2.10B`, the low-complementarity group's lead **−3** is **+10.66**, with the published pointwise interval **[4.23, 17.09]**; in `2.10A`, the corresponding all-occupation lead is **+7.79 [3.77, 11.81]**. Several other pre-period intervals also exclude zero. This is **not a formal joint rejection**: there are many leads, their covariance is unavailable, and pointwise intervals are not simultaneous bands. It does mean that “no evidence of pretrends” would be too strong. Joint tests, power, alternative reference periods, exposure-specific trends, placebo shocks and sensitivity to deviations from parallel trends remain needed. The displayed figure was also visually checked against the workbook.

The report's roughly 20% headline concerns relative **listing demand** for selected exposed/substitutable occupations. It is not a developer-specific headcount decline, a layoff count or an individual hiring probability. Likewise, changing experience shares can coexist with declines across all experience groups. The inspected Annex does not fully pin ad deduplication, whether monthly totals are newly posted flows or active stocks, repost treatment, zero-count handling, listing-to-vacancy multiplicity or frozen source-site coverage. Those details matter before converting counts into job creation. The paper's assertion that some estimates are lower bounds does not by itself prove the sign of all selection or identification biases.

## 2. The 2026 update: direct Indian adoption, different international-demand evidence

The [India survey catalog](https://microdata.worldbank.org/catalog/8552), [implementation report](https://microdata.worldbank.org/catalog/8552/download/359726) and [questionnaire](https://microdata.worldbank.org/catalog/8552/download/359725) are public. Version `IND_2026_WBES-OS_v01_M` was documented in August 2026. The India follow-up interviewed **1,355 establishments by telephone in March–April**, drawn from completed baseline interviews. Its universe is registered establishments with at least five employees in specified manufacturing, construction, trade and service sectors. It is not all enterprises, all workers, informal firms, solo developers or public-sector employment.

The implementation report chains baseline weights with **baseline completed interviews divided by follow-up completed interviews within two AI-intensity strata**. This addresses a stratum-level composition margin. It does not demonstrate that response is independent of adoption or performance within strata. Baseline interviews totaled 10,479 across April 2025–April 2026; **1,355/10,479 is not a response rate** without the eligible/contacted follow-up frame and timing. The chapter's cross-country sentence lists nine economies, excludes two, but says the retained 4,205 firms cover six economies. That country-count discrepancy was visually confirmed and remains unresolved; India and US component samples are separately stated as 1,355 and 401.

The [2026 workbook](https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/8ad688ae83bb2966f2ae7f2316431df3-0310012026/related/SAEU-October-2026-Charts-Chapter-2.xlsx), sheet `2.4.A`, reports weighted “any AI” rates **23.36% India and 42.68% US**. “Any AI” includes rule-based/traditional chatbots and general-purpose tools as well as advanced agents or automation. It does not mean 23.36% have autonomous AI production systems. The sophistication index combines multiple standardized components using inverse-covariance weights; its shifted scale is not a literal percent of potential AI capacity. Expected sales-per-worker improvements are answers to hypothetical faster versus slower AI-development scenarios holding employment fixed, not realized productivity gains.

The questionnaire asks AI adopters whether they hired, laid off, slowed/froze hiring or reassigned workers “due to” AI. It separately asks about attempted AI-skilled hiring in the last three months, including developer, applied, cloud and responsible-AI skills. These are valuable direct questions, but self-attributed adjustments are not randomized causal effects, yes/no establishment counts are not worker counts, and skip patterns change denominators. This pass did not calculate unweighted public-variable frequencies as representative population estimates or access respondent microdata. The catalog routes data access to an external repository; no login or account was used. Published chapter adoption rates are verified, while weighted hiring/layoff totals remain an open analysis question.

### Export linkage can expand while labor intensity changes

The newer chapter links Lightcast and US LinkUp ads to FactSet supplier/customer/partner relationships using a two-stage fuzzy name match. Of **435,249 Lightcast firms**, **9,443** match FactSet and **2,031** have US connections. These selected, generally larger/publicly visible firms are not representative of all Indian businesses. Relationship counts lack the transaction value, labor content and profitability of each link. They cannot be converted to export revenue, net jobs or jobs available remotely from India.

Firm/month regressions fix pre-ChatGPT exposure and relationship status, with firm and year-month effects. AI-posting-share equations use OLS; relationship counts use Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood; uncertainty generally clusters by firm. Industry exposure imports **US 2019 occupation shares**, explicitly for consistency, so the regional outcome data are local but the production structure inside the exposure measure is still partly projected from the US. Shared industry shocks and exposure-level dependence merit checks beyond firm clustering.

Annex Table A.2.2.6 estimates an all-firm AI-posting-share association of **0.0030 (SE 0.0013; 38,987 firm-months)** per exposure standard deviation. It does not show a comparably precise additional hiring advantage for firms with US supplier/customer/partner connections: their interaction terms and reported total effects are individually imprecise. A rising AI share does not establish rising total hiring. Separate relationship-count estimates are more positive for supplier/partner links. This supports an expansion channel worth testing, not a causal claim that AI raises aggregate Indian developer employment. The regressions still mostly end in **March 2025**, despite the October 2026 publication date.

## 3. Earlier Indian platform evidence: actual local ads, a different technology and population

The current Copestake et al. PDF supersedes the February 2023 draft for this pass. It studies a random 80% sample of one large platform's 2010–19 ads; the company is **explicitly unnamed**, so this audit does not identify it from third-party descriptions. The claimed approximately 60% share concerns online vacancies, not all Indian hiring. Recruitment and actual hiring occur offline. Salary ranges are compulsory in this platform's template, an advantage over the newer Lightcast pay subset; they remain offers, with midpoint/range and CPI-deflation assumptions. Its average non-AI offered-pay result is driven largely by a changed occupational mix; controlling that mix removes statistically significant within-occupation declines. It should not be described as observed wage cuts for every continuing worker.

The main IV sample is incumbent, domestic, nonremote, permanent service establishments posting in both 2010–12 and 2017–19. **IT services and education are excluded** to focus on AI users. Thus its negative estimates cannot automatically become an estimate for IT vendors, all software developers, startups or cross-border remote work. Software occupations inside finance and other user industries can still appear. First AI-related advertising proxies adoption; purchased AI and internal upskilling need not appear. Exact repost/dedup rules were not established.

The instrument combines baseline establishment occupational posting shares with Webb patent/task exposure. City, industry and baseline posting-size-decile effects and firm-clustered uncertainty are explicit. Table 5.1's fully controlled non-AI specification has **22,244 establishments**, first-stage **F=45.43**, and coefficient **−8.064 (SE 2.282)** on an instrumented adoption dummy. Its scale is a log-growth response per unit change in predicted adoption probability; the paper describes an approximately 8.1-percentage-point lower growth rate for a one-percentage-point higher probability. It is not an observed 8.1% fall across Indian employment.

Robustness is substantive: occupation controls, alternative windows, exclusion of the 2019 finance shock, firm effects, alternative intensive-margin measures, selection bounds, alternative estimators and shock-correlated standard errors are considered. However, predetermined shares do not alone prove exclusion. Baseline task mix can predict later demand through digitalization, clients or sectoral restructuring. The alternative Felten instrument gives weaker first stages and does **not** yield significant overall posting-growth effects, though some composition/pay patterns persist. The district analyses also find no robust aggregate posting effect, with weak first stages; these are important limits, not proof that all effects are zero.

Appendix B.1 explicitly says a genuinely prior period for its main long difference is unavailable. Its “pretrend” check uses year-on-year growth from 2014 onward in prose, while Table B.2 says 2013–19—overlapping the AI takeoff. That is not a clean all-pretreatment falsification. A separate propensity-weighted event study uses first-ad timing and balanced windows, with an imputation-estimator robustness check; matching observables still does not randomize unobserved restructuring or anticipation.

Two exact sample counts also require reconciliation **within this June version**: Table 2.1 reports 1,902,969 restricted ads, while Appendix Table A.2's final row reports 1,533,481 for the same stated period/filter, both with 24,485 establishments and 5,605 firms. Both tables were visually checked. This is not transferred criticism of an older draft, and it does not by itself invalidate Table 5.1. It prevents claiming that the exact underlying ad sample has been independently reproduced. The paper's model converting vacancy flows to implied employment additionally assumes fill/separation behavior; it is not observed payroll employment.

## Conditional preparation implications and falsifiers

For an unknown-CV developer, this evidence changes emphasis rather than supplying hiring odds. Formal Indian AI demand is directly observed, so applied-AI learning is not justified solely by a US forecast. Yet its coexistence with reduced non-AI ad growth makes **generic coding speed or an AI title insufficient proof of durable value**. A defensible preparation choice links existing software/data foundations to a real workflow, tests reliability/cost and demonstrates operational or customer responsibility. The existing preparation/selection gates should decide which requirement is reachable; no new assumed tenure, degree, work authorization or mastery level follows here.

Domain integration is a competing route, not automatic protection: the older evidence also affects analytical and communication tasks. Export/GCC work is another channel to examine, but more customer links do not remove client concentration, domestic-location or contractual access constraints. None of these sources establishes remote eligibility or the returns to a particular certificate.

The developer-specific inference would weaken if results disappear within stable employer/platform panels, under India-specific task mappings, after plausible differential trend controls, or when actual hires/pay replace advertised demand. It would strengthen with reproducible dictionary/dedup rules, frozen coverage, India-only and city/industry estimates, supported pretrends, credible adoption timing, spillover checks and linked hiring/earnings outcomes. For an individual's route, independently verified work transfer and attainable employer requirements are more informative than multiplying a regional premium by their salary.

Public chart aggregates were downloaded and inspected; proprietary ads, respondent data and complete estimation code were not reproduced. The source/claim matrix preserves hashes, windows, these gaps and the difference between observed, modelled and causally assumed quantities. Original reports, taxonomy and role snapshots remain unchanged.
